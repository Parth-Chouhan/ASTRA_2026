Lunar Landing Site Evaluator
Updated workflow with the official resources, richer metric visuals, a 3D sky dome and a relay-satellite simulation
1. What changed in this version
The hackathon gives four official resources. Using them properly makes the project faster to build and easier to defend:
JPL Horizons becomes the main data source. It already calculates the Sun and Earth as seen from any point on the Moon, so you no longer have to set up SPICE kernels and frame maths overnight. SPICE becomes an optional cross-check.
Quickmap and Moon Trek give you site coordinates, terrain context and a visual way to confirm your results. They also feed the terrain stretch goal.
The CSA Moon Exploration portal gives you real mission context for the Explorer mode and the pitch.
New in this update (all built on the same data, no extra API calls):
Richer metric visuals: gauges for sunlit % and Earth-visible %, a timeline strip, a "both available" bar and a launch-slip calendar (section 7).
3D sky dome: a view from the lander, with the Sun and Earth placed by their azimuth and elevation and a time slider (section 8).
Relay-satellite simulation: an example satellite orbit shown on the dome, with pass and coverage metrics (section 9).
Everything else stays as in the overnight plan: static JSON, a React frontend, 3 people plus AI agents, about 10 to 12 hours. The new features are gated: they start only after the core numbers are validated (see the timeline).

6. Step details
Step 1: Choose sites (Quickmap, Moon Trek)
Open Quickmap and find each candidate site. Read its latitude and longitude from the tool rather than from memory, and write down which source you used.
Suggested set: Apollo 11 (equatorial reference), Chandrayaan-3 landing site (Indian mission), Malapert massif, Shackleton crater rim, Mons Mouton (polar targets).
Take one screenshot of each site's terrain in Quickmap or Moon Trek for the site card.
Step 2: Fetch Sun and Earth positions (Horizons)
In the Horizons web form, set the observer location to a point on the Moon by coordinates (longitude, latitude, altitude) and choose the Sun as the target. Select azimuth and elevation as the output, a 2-hour step and a short date range. Copy the settings it shows.
Repeat with Earth as the target.
Give those settings to your agent to reproduce through the Horizons API from Python. A request looks roughly like this (check parameter names in the Horizons API documentation):
https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api?format=json
&COMMAND='10'            (10 = Sun, 399 = Earth)
&EPHEM_TYPE='OBSERVER'
&CENTER='coord@301'      (a point on the Moon)
&COORD_TYPE='GEODETIC'
&SITE_COORD='lon,lat,alt_km'
&START_TIME=...&STOP_TIME=...&STEP_SIZE='2h'
&QUANTITIES='4'          (azimuth and elevation)

Cache every response to disk so a failed network at 3 AM does not cost you the data.
Fetch a year in chunks (for example one month per request) to avoid size limits, wait briefly between requests, and join the chunks.
Write everything to data.json in the format below.
{ "sites": [ {
"id": "apollo11", "name": "Apollo 11", "lat": 0.67, "lon": 23.47,
"source": "Quickmap", "image": "apollo11.png",
"start": "2026-01-01T00:00:00Z", "step_hours": 2,
"sun_elev": [ ... ], "earth_elev": [ ... ]
} ] }

Step 3: Metrics (same as before)
Sunlit % and Earth-visible %: share of time steps with elevation above 0 degrees (flat horizon assumed; state this).
Longest darkness in hours, and time to next sunset or Earthset from the chosen date.
Viability colour (green / yellow / red) from a simple rule shown on screen. Power proxy = sin(Sun elevation), labelled as a rough indicator.
Step 4: Frontend views
Site dropdown + Sun and Earth elevation chart with a horizon line at 0 degrees.
Metrics cards and colour rating.
Date slider and time-to-sunset readout.
Comparison of two sites or two dates.
Date heatmap for launch-slip planning.
Site card with the Quickmap or Moon Trek screenshot and the coordinate source.
Explorer mode: plain-language explanations plus CSA facts with source links.
Step 5 (stretch only): Terrain horizon
If everything above works before hour 8, try one polar site. Check whether Moon Trek or Quickmap lets you export an elevation grid around the site, then have an agent calculate the highest terrain angle for each compass direction and draw it as a skyline behind the Sun and Earth tracks. If the export is awkward, skip it. A flat horizon, clearly labelled, is acceptable.

8. Feature: 3D sky dome (view from the lander)
Idea: instead of drawing Earth and the Sun as 3D models, show what an astronaut standing at the site would see: a horizon ring, the Sun and Earth positioned in the sky, and a time slider that makes them rise and set. This directly shows the question the planner cares about, how high each body is above the local horizon.
What the user sees
A flat ground disc with a horizon ring and compass marks (N, E, S, W), seen from a camera standing in the middle.
The Sun (yellow) and Earth (blue) as discs placed in the sky. If they are below the ring, they are hidden and drawn dimmed or shown in a small "below horizon" list.
Faint track lines showing where each body travels over the selected day or lunar day.
A time slider and play button. Dragging the slider moves the Sun and Earth (and the satellite from section 9) together.
A label reading "Sizes enlarged for visibility, not to scale" (the Sun is about 0.5 degrees wide and Earth about 2 degrees when seen from the Moon).
How to build it
Step 1: 2D version first. A polar plot (Plotly polar or SVG): the angle is azimuth, the distance from the edge is elevation. It uses only the data you already have. This is the safe version to ship.
Step 2: 3D upgrade with Three.js (npm install three). Convert each azimuth and elevation to a point on a sphere around the camera using the formulas below.
Add a ground disc, a horizon ring, discs for the Sun and Earth, OrbitControls so the user can look around, and a light fog or dark background for the sky.
Read the slider's time step, look up that step's azimuth and elevation in data.json, and move the discs. Use a texture only if you have checked its licence; plain colours are fine.
x =  cos(elevation) * sin(azimuth)     (east)
y =  sin(elevation)                    (up)
z = -cos(elevation) * cos(azimuth)     (north is -z)
position = distance * (x, y, z)        (same distance for all bodies)

Azimuth is measured from north towards east, in radians when you use JavaScript's Math functions.
Safe fallback: if Three.js causes trouble, the 2D polar view is already a complete feature. Do not spend more than about an hour on the 3D version.
9. Feature: relay-satellite simulation
Why: at many sites Earth is below the horizon for days, so direct communication fails. Real missions use relay satellites for this. A simulated relay on the sky dome shows how contact could continue, using the same elevation maths as for the Sun and Earth. It needs no extra data and runs in the browser.
Simple model: one example orbit
A circular polar orbit about 100 km above the surface (user can change altitude and start angle with sliders).
The orbit plane stays fixed in space while the Moon slowly turns beneath it (one turn in about 27.3 days).
Because the orbit time is only about two hours, the satellite needs a finer time step than your 2-hour Horizons data. Calculate it in the browser every 1 to 2 minutes, only for the selected site and the selected window (for example 1 day, or the whole year on demand).
R = 1737.4 km (Moon radius);  a = R + altitude   (e.g. 1837.4 km)
T = 2*pi*sqrt(a^3 / 4902.8)      -> about 7,070 s (about 118 min)
theta = 2*pi * t / T            (satellite angle along its orbit)
inertial position: x = a*cos(theta), y = 0, z = a*sin(theta)
Moon turns: phi = 2*pi * t / (27.32 days)
Moon-fixed:  x' =  x*cos(phi) + y*sin(phi)
y' = -x*sin(phi) + y*cos(phi),   z' = z
site position = R*(cos(lat)cos(lon), cos(lat)sin(lon), sin(lat))
vector = satellite - site
elevation = asin( dot(vector, up) / length(vector) )
visible if elevation > 0 degrees

4902.8 km^3/s^2 is the Moon's gravitational parameter. Check it, and the other constants, against a trusted source before you use them.
New metrics
Passes per day and length of each pass.
Longest gap with no satellite in view.
Combined coverage = share of time with Earth directly visible OR a satellite overhead.
Show these as extra gauges and as a third bar on the timeline strip.
What to expect (use as a sanity check)
Near the pole, a polar-orbit satellite should appear on almost every orbit, about every 118 minutes. With a 100 km altitude it should stay in view roughly 12 minutes per pass.
At an equatorial site, coverage should come in multi-day windows separated by long gaps, because the Moon's rotation carries the site into and out of the orbit plane. If your simulation gives the same result everywhere, something is wrong.
Limits to state honestly
It is an example orbit, not a real mission's relay design. Label it "Example relay orbit" in the app.
This version does not check whether the satellite can itself see Earth, which the Moon can block. State this as future work.
Real orbits drift (the Moon's uneven gravity), which is ignored here.
If you have spare time, Horizons may offer real lunar orbiter data. I could not check, so treat it as an optional extra.
11. Validation using the resources
Check
What you should see
South pole Sun height
Stays within about 1.5 degrees of the horizon all year, since the Moon's axis is barely tilted. A site with Sun at 20 degrees means a wrong coordinate or sign.
Near side vs far side
Earth is nearly fixed in the sky at near-side sites and never visible on the far side.
Equatorial site (Apollo 11)
Sun rises and sets about every 29.5 Earth days, reaching high elevation at local noon.
Horizons web form vs your script
Pick one site and time, read the value in the web form, and compare with your JSON. They should match.
Sky dome orientation
North, east, south and west marks match the data: a site where the Sun is at azimuth near 0 degrees should show the Sun toward the N mark.
Satellite period
About 118 minutes at 100 km altitude, matching the formula in section 9. Passes near the pole should repeat about every 2 hours.
Satellite visibility rule
Elevation is always between -90 and 90 degrees, and a pass is only counted when elevation is above 0.
Quickmap sense check
For a polar site, check the terrain in Quickmap: high rims should match sites your tool says have more sunlight. If Quickmap offers an illumination view for your date, compare it to your chart.

Optional cross-check if you have spare time: compute one site yourself with SPICE (spiceypy) and compare it with the Horizons numbers.

Agent 2 (Frontend):

Agent 3 (Metrics and heatmap):
Write a JavaScript module that takes the elevation arrays and returns sunlit %, Earth-visible %, longest continuous darkness in hours, hours until next sunset or Earthset from a given step, and a per-day sunlit % array for a calendar heatmap. Add unit tests with small hand-made arrays.
Agent 4 (3D sky dome):
Using Three.js in my React app, build a sky dome view from the lander's viewpoint. Place the Sun and Earth using azimuth and elevation from data.json with x = cos(el)*sin(az), y = sin(el), z = -cos(el)*cos(az). Add a ground disc, a horizon ring with N/E/S/W labels, OrbitControls, a time slider and play button, and a note that sizes are not to scale. First build a 2D polar plot version of the same view, then upgrade to 3D.
Agent 5 (satellite simulation):
Write a JavaScript module that simulates a circular polar lunar orbit at a given altitude, using Moon radius 1737.4 km and gravitational parameter 4902.8 km^3/s^2. Given a site latitude and longitude and a time range, compute the satellite elevation every 2 minutes (the Moon rotates once per 27.32 days under a fixed orbit plane) and return passes (start, end, maximum elevation), the longest gap, and combined coverage with an Earth-visible array I supply. Include unit tests, for example a pass roughly every 118 minutes near the south pole.
13. Fallbacks
Horizons API is slow or rejects requests: run the cases in the web form and paste the output tables into files, then have an agent parse them. Reduce to 5 sites and a shorter date range.
Observer-on-Moon settings confuse you: test in the web form first and only automate after one case matches your expectations.
Moon Trek or Quickmap exports fail: use screenshots only, and skip the terrain horizon.
SPICE option: if Horizons is unusable, fall back to spiceypy with the Moon-fixed frame, as in the previous overnight plan.
3D dome is slow or buggy: keep the 2D polar sky view. It is a complete feature.
Satellite results look wrong: drop it from the demo, and mention it as future work rather than showing wrong numbers.
Behind schedule at hour 8: skip the 3D and satellite features and polish the core gauges and timeline.
14. Pitch: show you used the official tools
Say that your numbers come from JPL Horizons and were checked against the Horizons web form and known polar behaviour.
Show a site card with a Quickmap or Moon Trek view next to the chart, and name the source.
Use a CSA fact in Explorer mode, for example a Canadian contribution to lunar exploration, with a link.
Be open about limits: flat horizon (unless you finished the terrain stretch), simple power proxy, Sun disk ignored.
Demo the sky dome: drag the slider to show the Sun and Earth rising and setting, then show how an example relay satellite fills the gap when Earth is hidden. Say clearly that the sizes are enlarged and the orbit is an example.
Use the timeline strip to tell the story: two sites with the same sunlit % can have very different longest darkness.
